# Vection and Visually Induced Motion Sickness — Study Notes

**Authors**: Behrang Keshavarz, Bernhard E. Riecke, Lawrence J. Hettinger, and Jennifer L. Campos.  
**Published**: Frontiers in Psychology **6:472**, April 20, 2015; DOI **10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00472**.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed narrative review; read the complete 11-page journal-layout article, including references and copyright statement. It contains no numbered figures or tables, no new participant experiment, and no meta-analysis.

## What It Is

A review of the relation between **vection**, the experience of illusory self-motion without actual movement through space, and **visually induced motion sickness (VIMS)**, motion-sickness-like symptoms driven strongly by visual stimulation with little or no physical movement. Simulator sickness, cybersickness, and gaming sickness overlap but are not necessarily identical: equipment may also supply physical motion and symptoms differ across contexts.

Its central distinction is between a factor being **associated with**, **necessary for**, and **sufficient for** sickness. Vection clearly occurs without VIMS, so is not sufficient. Whether VIMS can occur without vection remains unresolved, partly because investigators disagree about whether eye strain and dizziness without perceived self-motion belong to VIMS or another symptom category.

## Mechanisms and Competing Accounts

- **Sensory conflict**: mismatches between or within visual, vestibular, and somatosensory information can produce symptoms. Visual evidence of acceleration can disagree with stationary-body signals. Constant-velocity self-motion need not create the same sustained conflict because vestibular signals principally register acceleration. The review does not turn this theoretical account into a universal safe-motion prescription.
- **Multisensory integration**: sensory capture, reliability-weighted integration, or ignoring an unreliable signal might reconcile conflicting inputs differently. The proposed application of these models to the vection–VIMS relationship is explicitly **not yet empirically tested** in the review.
- **Postural instability**: loss of postural control is proposed to precede and cause sickness, rather than merely follow it. Sway and vection can be related, but their temporal ordering varies; an obligatory causal chain from conscious vection through sway to sickness is unsupported.
- **Eye movement hypothesis**: optokinetic nystagmus elicited by moving patterns may contribute to sickness through proposed vagal activation. Fixation can reduce eye movements and symptoms without reducing vection. Changes in speed can also change sensory conflict and posture, so these explanations are not isolated or mutually exclusive.

A shared neural cause is not established. At publication, the authors knew of no study measuring neural activity for vection and VIMS simultaneously.

## Empirical Evidence Reported by the Review

These are **secondary summaries**, not independent readings of the underlying studies. The review does not consistently provide full sample sizes, participant demographics, confidence intervals, or standardized between-condition effect sizes; unavailable details should not be invented.

| Study / exposure | Participants and measures reported here | Result and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Hettinger et al. (1990), fixed-base flight simulator | Subjective vection and VIMS ratings; total sample and demographics not supplied | **8 of 9** participants reporting VIMS also reported vection; among **5** without vection, **1** reported VIMS. Strong co-occurrence is not an exception-free prerequisite. |
| Smart et al. (2002), furnished room moving around stationary people | **12** participants; reported vection and VIMS | **11** reported vection; **3 of those 11** also reported VIMS. Only one participant lacked vection, making necessity a weak inference. |
| Diels et al. (2007), contracting/expanding random-dot optic flow on a wide-field display | Subjective sickness and vection ratings; sample not supplied | Positive correlations **up to r = .70**. This is association under particular stimuli, not a pooled effect. |
| Keshavarz and Berti (2014), horizontally moving black/white stripe patterns | Subjective vection and VIMS ratings; sample not supplied | **r = .47**, reported as **not statistically significant**. |
| Keshavarz et al. (2014c), rotating stimulus | Subjective ratings; sample not supplied | Everyone reporting VIMS also reported vection, but magnitudes correlated only weakly, **up to r = .20**. |
| Ji et al. (2009), independently rotating central and peripheral striped fields | Curved projection display; sickness measured with a **7-point nausea scale**; sample not supplied | Opposing center/periphery motion eliminated reported vection without reducing reported VIMS relative to same-direction motion. The review calls for further support and generalization, and later questions whether these are VIMS or oculomotor-specific symptoms. |

Other dissociations reinforce the problem with treating vection strength as a sickness meter: dual-axis rotation increases sickness without increasing vection; a stationary reference background reduces sickness without reducing vection; denser optic flow increases vection without changing sickness. Fixation can lower VIMS while preserving or even strengthening vection. The review supplies no common effect magnitude across these studies.

The authors cite repeated-exposure and acclimation research as promising for reducing simulator sickness. One driving-simulator comparison combines a delayed test with a second acclimation session, so the account cannot isolate delay from additional practice. These findings do not justify requiring ordinary interface users to endure symptoms until they adapt.

## Measurement and Reporting Admissions

Subjective onset, duration, and strength measures require a clear experiential definition. Vection scales often lack shared anchors or practice experiences; even standardized sickness questionnaires are not straightforwardly comparable across people and sessions. Physiological measures do not reliably replace subjective reports.

Studies often measure only one phenomenon, omit sickness data, or exclude participants when symptoms develop. Variable exclusion thresholds and attrition can bias the apparent relationship. Compiled sickness scores can also conceal different relations for nausea, disorientation, and oculomotor disturbances. The authors recommend measuring both phenomena in the same people, reporting symptom categories and exclusions transparently, and experimentally seeking dissociations rather than presuming causation from co-occurrence.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Do not wait for a report of felt self-motion before taking symptoms seriously.** The review's dispute over terminology does not negate dizziness, nausea, or eye strain.
2. **Treat sensory conflict as an explanatory family, not a settled single cause.** Posture, eye movements, exposure conditions, and individual differences can contribute.
3. **Measure symptoms directly rather than using immersion as a safety proxy.** Strong vection can be comfortable; weak or absent reported vection does not prove absence of adverse symptoms.
4. **Avoid unnecessary self-motion simulation.** The authors recommend asking whether vection serves the task at all; ordinary interface applications are an extrapolation from simulator and perceptual research.

## Questions and Limitations

- Narrative selection has no reported systematic search, screening flow, risk-of-bias assessment, or pooled sample/effect. Studies differ in stimuli, procedures, measures, and symptom definitions. Some report positive correlations and others fail to find them; this is heterogeneous evidence, not a consistently replicated universal dose–response relation.
- The review alternates between describing vection as inherently involving sensory conflict and explaining that constant-velocity vection can involve little sustained conflict. Read “conflict” with its timing, type, and magnitude, rather than as one binary property.
- **Internal wording error**: the application section says displays capable of inducing vection “can also be prone to induce vection,” apparently repeating the word where the surrounding argument concerns VIMS. Preserve the argument without treating that sentence as an additional finding.
- The Ji result is presented as evidence for symptoms without vection, then qualified by uncertainty about the VIMS label. The final summary still treats necessity as possible, not demonstrated or refuted conclusively. The early Hettinger summary also includes one VIMS report without vection.
- The source does not test web-page transitions, reduced-motion alternatives, small looping indicators, safe pulse rates, or a clinical motion-sensitive user population as one cohort. It cannot validate a safe animation size or frequency for interfaces. Replication of the key no-vection dissociation remains a requested next step in this 2015 account, not an established outcome here.
