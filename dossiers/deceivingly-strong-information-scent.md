# Deceivingly Strong Information Scent Costs Sales — Study Notes

**Author**: Jakob Nielsen.  
**Published**: Nielsen Norman Group, **August 1, 2004**; web print captured October 14, 2026. The live page confirms both date and canonical URL.  
**Status**: Practitioner interpretation of first-hand qualitative usability observations, not a complete empirical report or controlled intervention. Read the complete **four-page PDF**, including its footer and visual layout, and the canonical article to recover text obscured by the print's privacy overlay. The article contains no study figures; related-content thumbnails are not evidence.

## What It Is

An important qualification to “make information scent stronger”: a clear, compelling path can make an incorrect destination seem exhaustive. When the expected answer is missing there, people may infer that the whole site lacks it rather than search elsewhere. The problem is the **relationship between competing cues and the actual information**, not simply that a link is too clear.

## Mechanism as an Idea

Information scent concerns what people expect to find after following a path. A task-relevant category and an apparently ideal title create a strong expectation. If that destination fails, a weaker or technical label elsewhere may not appear to be a meaningful alternative. The person's local failure becomes an inference about global absence.

A cross-reference can bridge organizational categories, but its existence alone is insufficient. Its wording must communicate how the destination answers the person's question. This is **goal-relative predictability**, not merely short wording, a precise technical term, or conspicuous styling.

## Observations / Admissions

### Teenagers seeking a weight-related answer

The article reports that **several teenage users** failed an assigned task: determine how much they could weigh without being considered overweight. On KidsHealth, **“What's the Right Weight for My Height?”**, prominently placed under **“Food & Fitness”** and appearing high in a weight search, looked like the correct destination but did not contain the requested answer.

Nielsen reports that users concluded the site lacked the information. A different article, **“Body Mass Index (BMI),”** discussed weight, height, and age and included charts answering the question. The first article already linked to it using **“body mass index,”** but users overlooked that low-scent link. Thus this is not simply missing cross-linking: the bridge existed but did not communicate its relevance to the task.

The source interprets the BMI wording as technical and intimidating, suggesting users would avoid it unless good at math. It supplies no math-skill measure or independent test of intimidation; this explanation is not an established causal mechanism. The health-content example concerns historical navigation, not evidence validating BMI as medical guidance for teenagers.

### Shoppers seeking a baby car seat

In an e-commerce usability study, users looked in an automotive section for a baby car seat. The product existed elsewhere without a cross-reference from the automotive category. Nielsen reports that users inferred the site did not sell it and that no sale resulted. The example supports a possible findability-to-abandonment pathway, **not a quantified revenue or conversion effect**.

### What the report does not provide

Neither example supplies total participant counts, demographics beyond teenagers in the first example, recruitment, task order, recording or analysis methods, precise failure rates, timings, uncertainty, or effect sizes. “Several” and “many other studies” cannot be converted into denominators or independent replications. The article reports recurring experience but supplies no inspectable replication results and no comparison showing how much revised labels or cross-references improve success.

## Recommendations and Rationale

- **Use labels that predict the destination in the user's terms.** A technically explicit term can still conceal a useful answer from the intended audience.
- **Cross-reference plausible alternative categories.** An organization's classification need not match the location in which people reasonably expect to find something.
- **Make the cross-reference itself meaningful.** A low-scent bridge can be overlooked even when placed inside the page users visit.
- **Inspect competing paths, not isolated labels.** A highly plausible wrong path can dominate a weaker correct path; making every cue vague is not the remedy.

These are reasoned recommendations grounded in the observations, not experimentally demonstrated redesign outcomes.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **A first click is not proof of a good promise.** Assess whether the destination actually satisfies what its label led people to expect.
2. **Study what people conclude after a dead end.** Abandonment can mean “the site does not have it,” not “I cannot operate the navigation.”
3. **Treat multiple reasonable classifications as a findability problem.** Preserve a route from where people expect an item, even when its primary home remains elsewhere.
4. **Measure success after the bridge.** A cross-link or click increase does not establish that the underlying question was answered or the purchase completed.

## Questions and Limitations

- The examples are historical and do not describe the current behavior of the named sites. The article's capture date is not its publication date.
- The title and closing warning about losing customers generalize beyond the reported evidence: no sales totals, conversion changes, or controlled before/after results are supplied.
- **Scope tension, not numerical inconsistency:** the article calls the weight-title microcontent explicit and effective while reporting task failure. It distinguishes an attractive promise from fulfillment, rather than showing that explicit wording is intrinsically harmful. The claim that a cross-reference is the solution is qualified by the first example already containing an overlooked technical cross-reference.
