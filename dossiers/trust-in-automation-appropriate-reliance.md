# Trust in Automation — Study Notes

**Authors**: John D. Lee and Katrina A. See.  
**Published**: Human Factors **46(1), Spring 2004, pp. 50–80**; received August 5, 2002; accepted September 4, 2003.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed integrative review and conceptual model. Read the complete **31-page publisher-layout PDF**, supplied as a Wayback copy of the author-hosted file, including four figures, Table 1, and references. The matching publisher record verifies DOI **10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392**.  

## What It Is

A synthesis connecting human–automation trust research with organizational, interpersonal, sociological, psychological, and neurological perspectives. Its design objective is **appropriate trust, not greater trust**.

The authors define trust as **the attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual's goals in a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability**. Reliance is behavior. Trust influences but does not determine it: someone may trust an aid yet lack time to configure it, or use an aid because alternatives are worse rather than because trust is high.

This is not a new trust experiment, a systematic meta-analysis, or a validated predictive implementation of the proposed model.

## Problem and Motivation

Complex, imperfect automation can exceed a person's ability to understand every operation or anticipate every situation. Trust can act as a decision heuristic under that uncertainty, but both **misuse through overreliance** and **disuse through underreliance** can compromise performance.

The source reviews aviation, process control, maritime operations, driving, target detection, information retrieval, and interface credibility. It asks what information and cognitive processes make trust track actual capability, and why apparently conflicting studies may reflect different functions, contexts, histories, and alternative ways of performing the task.

## Mechanism as an Idea

### Beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behavior are not interchangeable

Figures 1 and 4 distinguish **information assimilation and belief formation → trust evolution → intention formation → reliance action**, with feedback from actual automation performance through its display. Workload, engagement effort, risk, self-confidence, time constraints, and configuration errors enter at different points. Context also changes the automation's capability, not just the person's opinion of it.

Calling observed delegation “trust” would obscure these intervening factors. Conversely, a questionnaire score alone cannot establish appropriate behavior or combined human–automation performance.

### Three qualities of appropriate trust

- **Calibration**: correspondence between trust and actual capability. Overtrust exceeds capability; distrust falls below it.
- **Resolution**: discrimination between different levels of capability. A person who trusts equally across substantially different performance states has poor resolution even if their overall average judgment is reasonable.
- **Specificity**: trust tracks the relevant **function, subfunction, or mode**, and the relevant **time and situation**. Trust in one subsystem should not automatically transfer to another; long-term averages can obscure momentary limitations.

Figure 2's diagonal and response ranges are conceptual illustrations, not data or a universal numerical conversion between a trust scale and reliability probability.

### Performance, process, and purpose

Table 1 maps dimensions from **14 cited accounts** onto three bases of trust:

| Basis | Question it answers | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | What does it do, and how reliably does it achieve my goal? | Outcomes and historical reliability are task- and situation-dependent. |
| **Process** | How does it work, and are its mechanisms suitable here? | Understanding can support trust without guaranteeing every output. |
| **Purpose** | Why was it built, and what use was intended? | Automation embodies designer intent; attributing human benevolence or motives to the machine can mislead. |

These are **levels of attributional abstraction, not mandatory developmental stages**. Users can begin with faith in the stated purpose before obtaining performance evidence. Inferences between purpose, process, and performance may support trust when coherent; contradictions can undermine it.

### Analytic, analogical, and affective processes

Trust can develop through deliberate analysis, category-based analogy or rules, and affective responses. Figure 3 emphasizes their interaction and the potential dominance of affect. The neurological evidence concerns emotion and decision making more broadly; it is not direct identification of a unique neural mechanism for trusting automation.

Reputation, informal discussion, social cues, and vivid presentation can influence trust even when they do not change actual capability. Consequently, a polished or humanlike interface may increase reliance without improving calibration.

### Reliance changes what a user can learn

The model is **closed-loop and history-dependent**. Disuse may prevent observation of successful operation and inhibit recovery of trust. Heavy reliance may reduce monitoring and hide occasional failures. Information-acquisition aids may remain observable without delegation; action automation, such as a controller operating a pump, may be harder to observe after returning to manual control.

Acute failures, chronic deficiencies, predictability, initial experience, and environmental change can therefore produce different trust trajectories. Average reliability alone is insufficient to characterize this process.

## Results / Admissions

There is **no new participant cohort or pooled effect size**. The following numbers belong to cited studies or analyses, not to an experiment conducted for this review. Most reviewed studies' participant counts, precise tests, and confidence intervals are absent from the article.

| Cited evidence | Task, sample information, and measures supplied | Finding and interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Singh, Molloy, and Parasuraman 1993 | Automation-failure monitoring under constant reliability; high versus low scores on a complacency-potential scale; sample counts not supplied | High-scoring individuals detected **53.4%**, versus **18.7%** for low-scoring individuals. A general propensity-to-trust measure is not a simple predictor of misuse. |
| Lee and Moray 1992/1994 | Simulated process control/pasteurization; delegation, subjective trust, self-confidence, time-series analysis; sample counts not supplied | Trust and self-confidence jointly related to manual versus automatic allocation. A dynamic equation accounted for **60–86% of variance** in overall reliance patterns; that is model fit within cited data, not a universal prediction accuracy. |
| Parasuraman et al. 1993, 1994; Molloy and Parasuraman 1996 | Engine-status monitoring plus tracking/fuel management; novice and experienced-pilot conditions and infrequent-fault extension | Highly reliable automation with competing manual work reduced detection; sole-task monitoring detected almost all failures. The review reports corroboration with experienced pilots and infrequent faults, not replication of the entire conceptual model. |
| Parasuraman, Mouloua, and Molloy 1996 | Multitask flight simulation; continuously automated monitoring versus **10 minutes** returned to manual monitoring | The manual interval substantially improved later failure detection. No effect magnitude or participant denominator is reproduced here. |
| Lewandowsky, Mundy, and Tan 2000 | Semiautomatic pasteurization; pump delegated either to automation or a supposed human, actually the same controller | Trust related more strongly to automation delegation; perceived judgments of one's own trustworthiness mattered for human delegation. Reciprocal social relationships cannot be assumed identical to automation relationships. |
| Yamagishi and Yamagishi 1994 | Interpersonal cross-national questionnaire; **1,136 Japanese and 501 American respondents** | Differences in generalized trust and relationship/reputation emphasis illustrate cultural context. This is not a direct sample of automation users or proof of fixed national automation behavior. |
| Fogg et al. 2001 | Online website-credibility survey, **more than 1,400 people** | “Real-world feel,” including organization photos and physical addresses, influenced credibility. This does not establish the truth of site claims or appropriate automation reliance. |

Other cited studies found that predictable faults could be accommodated, that trust could remain specific to a faulty controller, and that incorrect cues or realistic imagery affected attention and reliance. The source does not supply enough study-level detail for a pooled reliability threshold or interface effect estimate.

Reported reliability levels at which trust dropped sharply range across studies from **90% to 70% to 60%**. The authors explicitly conclude **no single reliability level** guarantees trust or produces disuse. Fault timing, consequences, predictability, expectations, and alternative manual capability matter.

### Recommendations versus demonstrations

The paper recommends showing past performance, understandable intermediate processes, intended purpose and operating range, and how situations change capability. It recommends evaluating calibration, resolution, and specificity, and carefully evaluating anthropomorphism. These are **tentative design, evaluation, and training implications** of the synthesis, not a controlled comparison validating the whole package.

It distinguishes **trustworthy** automation—efficient and reliable—from **trustable** automation—supporting appropriate adaptive reliance. A simpler but somewhat less capable system might outperform a more capable opaque one at the joint-system level, but that tradeoff is proposed for investigation, not demonstrated as a general superiority of simple algorithms.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Make the success criterion appropriate reliance, not a higher trust score.** Measure whether decisions fit actual capability and consequences alongside attitudes.
2. **Make trust local.** Provide evidence relevant to the current function, mode, and context rather than borrowing confidence from the whole product or an unrelated successful task.
3. **Distinguish output confidence from a basis for trust.** Capability history, mechanisms, intended use, and contextual limits answer different questions.
4. **Account for experience that the interaction prevents.** A system that cannot be inspected without fully delegating may make it difficult to rebuild justified trust after failure.
5. **Treat humanlike polish as a possible confound.** Persuasive social cues can amplify reliance without improving capability; inspect whether they support or distort expectations.

For AI-agent supervision these are **extrapolations**: assess whether people delegate appropriately, can inspect consequential evidence, and notice context-specific limitations rather than treating fluent output or general confidence as proof of correctness. The paper does not study contemporary generative models, benchmark calibration methods, or modern coding agents.

## Questions and Limitations

- This broad narrative review lacks a reproducible systematic-search protocol, complete per-study methods, a meta-analysis, or validation of the integrated model as a whole. Its original figures are explanatory diagrams, not estimated causal paths.
- Neurological lesion, gambling, and cooperation findings support the role of emotion in decisions; transferring them to trust in automation remains theoretical. Claims that affect dominates analytic processes should retain that evidence boundary.
- Automation lacks the intentionality and reciprocal social exchange assumed by many interpersonal theories. Purpose can mean designer intent or user attribution, not demonstrated machine motives.
- The discussion often simplifies reliance into engagement/disengagement; the authors acknowledge real automation can involve many modes and graded reliance.
- Little direct automation research then addressed cultural differences, context-sensitive capability displays, and multiperson management. These are **2004 research gaps**, not claims about the entire later literature.
- The review itself records conflicts with fixed-stage accounts of trust: purpose-based trust can precede performance evidence. It resolves this conceptually rather than declaring one chronological sequence universal.
- An internal wording inconsistency on p. 71 calls the pasteurization rating dimensions **“purpose, process, and product”**, while the definitions and subsequent discussion use **performance**. On p. 72, the process rating is said to be most influenced by a fault “as compared with the process or purpose dimensions,” repeating the dimension being compared. Preserve these as unresolved editorial inconsistencies rather than inferring an exact corrected statistical comparison.
- The discussion of speech cues briefly says **“To maximize trust”** when summarizing cited persuasion work, while the paper's own recommendation is to promote **appropriate**, not maximal, trust. That local sentence is not the article's design objective.
- **Redistribution evidence**: the PDF states **“Copyright © 2004, Human Factors and Ergonomics Society. All rights reserved.”** No explicit redistribution grant appears. Author hosting and a Wayback copy do not authorize a public PDF archive; retain the raw file privately.
