# Driven by notifications – exploring the effects of badge notifications on user experience — Study Notes

**Authors**: Nicola Bartoli and Simone Benedetto; Jakala Experience Lab, JAKALA, Milan, Italy.  
**Published**: *PLOS ONE* **17(6), e0270888**, **June 30, 2022**; received November 5, 2021, accepted June 21, 2022; DOI **10.1371/journal.pone.0270888**.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed remote between-subjects experiment. Read the complete **nine-page publisher PDF**, including both figures, both tables, supporting-information declaration, and references; visually inspected the usage and click-proportion figures. The linked supplementary dataset was not analyzed.

## What It Is

An experiment testing whether a single **notification badge on an app icon changes which app people choose first** on a static smartphone home screen. Across all fifteen apps, the badge-bearing version received a higher first-click proportion than the same app when a different app carried the badge.

The durable finding is **choice redirection by a small status cue**, not demonstrated improvement in user experience. The task did not establish an objectively correct destination, actual unread content, or an ongoing task that deserved priority.

## Problem and Motivation

Badges combine a visually conspicuous mark with learned meaning: something awaits attention. The authors propose **salience** and **perceived urgency** as explanations for their effect. These are theoretical interpretations. The experiment neither independently manipulates salience and urgency nor measures gaze, perceived urgency, automatic processing, anxiety, or comprehension.

A badge can therefore be consequential without being a neutral reflection of importance. Whether the redirected action serves the person's goal is a separate question from whether it attracts a click.

## Study Design

### Participants and assignment

An online panel recruited **1,095 self-declared smartphone users**, **55% women**, mean age **38 years, SD 10**. These demographics describe recruitment; separate retained-sample demographics are not supplied. Participants were unaware of the aims and received a small financial compensation, amount unspecified.

The researchers distributed participants among **15 groups of 73**, one group per badge location. They excluded **86** people for clicking outside app icons, leaving **N = 1,009**. Table 1 shows unequal retained badge-condition groups, **58–73**, so “equinumerous” applies to initial allocation rather than analysis. The source describes distribution among groups but does not explicitly state a random-assignment procedure.

The study reports written informed consent and JAKALA Experience Lab Ethics Committee approval. It declares no specific funding and no competing interests.

### Stimulus and task

Each participant viewed one static **Android home screen with fifteen app icons and exactly one badge**, displaying **1**. Layout and apps were held constant; only the badged app changed. Apps were selected to resemble use in Italy, combining default phone functions with social, messaging, shopping, and entertainment services. The source cites Android's roughly **75% Italian market share** as the platform rationale, not as a sample characteristic.

The instruction was **“Where would you click first on this screen?”** The test ended after one click. This was a free-choice first-impression task, not repeated natural app opening, responding to real messages, or finding an app specified by an external goal. The average **90-second session** included instructions, the click, usage questions, and demographics; it is not a reported click-response latency.

A post-task five-point questionnaire assessed ordinary app usage. Figure 1 shows WhatsApp, Telephone, Facebook, and Gmail used most frequently, and LinkedIn, Shazam, Teams, and Just Eat least. These are self-reported habits, not randomized familiarity conditions.

### Comparison and analysis

For **each app separately**, the researchers compared its first-click proportion in the group where it carried the badge against the pooled fourteen groups where another app carried the badge. Consequently the no-badge comparison means **“this app has no badge while a competing app does”**, not a completely badge-free screen.

Each participant contributed a binary selected/not-selected observation for each app, with just one selected app. Within-app comparisons involve distinct participants; the fifteen app-level outcomes share participants and are not fifteen independent replications.

The authors use Kruskal–Wallis tests across badge-location groups and adjusted Mann–Whitney planned contrasts for badge versus no badge. Table 2 reports all omnibus tests and contrasts as **p < .001**, with **H(14, N = 1009)** and contrast **Z** statistics. It does not give numerical confidence bounds or standardized effect sizes. Figure 2 depicts confidence intervals without specifying their confidence level or calculation in its caption.

The raw absolute differences range **+9.1 to +41.5 percentage points**. All fifteen planned contrasts favor the badge. Illustrative statistics are **WhatsApp H = 64.07, Z = 6.53**; **Telephone H = 143.22, Z = 11.75**; and **Gmail H = 35.91, Z = 4.30**, all reported **p < .001**. The paper's brief statement that gender and age interactions yielded no results supplies no interaction coefficients, test statistics, or uncertainty; it is not evidence of demographic equivalence.

The strongest badge click proportion is not universal: WhatsApp remains much more often chosen than many alternatives even without its own badge. The study demonstrates redirection across this app set, not equalization of app attractiveness or the irrelevance of prior habits.

The authors infer that badges capture attention and stimulate action and discuss salience and urgency biases. **Clicks were directly observed; attention capture and urgency were not directly measured**. Nothing in the experiment establishes that the selected action was harmful, beneficial, important, understood, or contrary to a pre-existing intention.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Treat badge placement as a choice intervention.** A small status mark can substantially redirect the first action, not merely decorate existing information.
2. **Do not equate click-through with better experience.** The study has no task-success, satisfaction, wellbeing, retention, or interruption-recovery outcome.
3. **Retain the comparator.** These differences arise when one app is singled out against competitors; they do not predict a screen full of badges or a badge-free baseline.
4. **Separate learned meaning from visual salience.** The design bundles both; it does not establish whether redness, the count, position, or unread-message expectations cause the observed effect.
5. **Require longitudinal evidence for habit claims.** One click on a static screen cannot demonstrate checking frequency, compulsive use, habituation, or repeated-warning fatigue.

## Questions and Limitations

- **Numerical/text inconsistencies**: the apparatus section says **nine** further apps were added to four defaults but lists **eleven**, consistent with fifteen total. Retained groups are unequal after exclusions despite repeated equal-group wording. Table 1's printed badge group sizes sum to **1,009**, agreeing with **1,095 − 86**; no sample-count mismatch remains once initial and retained allocation are distinguished.
- **Reporting ambiguity**: the results describe comparison with “the rest of the apps with no badge (n = 14)”; Table 1 clarifies person-level pooled comparator samples of **936–951**, not fourteen participants. “Frequency of clicks” means a first-choice proportion here, not repeated clicking by a person.
- **Mechanism and ecological validity**: no gaze/attention measure, actual notification content, time pressure, real task priority, or personal home-screen configuration was tested. Italy-oriented app selection does not establish cross-cultural generality; nationality details and randomization method are not provided.
- **Confounding and uncertainty**: badge count, color, and location were not independently varied; app positions were held fixed rather than counterbalanced. The “adjusted” contrast method is named but its adjustment is not operationally detailed in the paper. Numerical confidence bounds and a specified interval level are absent.
- **Replication**: consistency across fifteen apps is within one experiment. No independent replication, repeated-exposure test, or naturalistic follow-up is established by this source. Supporting data are linked but were not reanalyzed for this ingest.
