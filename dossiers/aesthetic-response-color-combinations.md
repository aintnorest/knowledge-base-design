# Aesthetic Response to Color Combinations — Study Notes

**Authors**: Karen B. Schloss and Stephen E. Palmer; University of California, Berkeley.  
**Published**: Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics **73, 551–571 (2011)**; published online **November 10, 2010**. DOI: **10.3758/s13414-010-0027-0**.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed empirical article; read the complete **21-page publisher-formatted, lab-hosted PDF**, including Figures 1–13 and the appendix table. Linked supplementary material is referenced by the paper but is not included in this supplied file.  

## What It Is

Four experiments within the Berkeley Color Project separate judgments often collapsed into “good color combinations”:

- **Pair preference**: how much someone likes the combination as a whole.
- **Pair harmony**: how well the colors go or belong together, independently of liking them.
- **Figural preference**: how much someone likes the foreground color against a particular background.
- **Similarity**, measured separately, tests whether harmony is merely another name for perceptual resemblance.

The central result is not a universal harmonious palette. Similar hues generally improve whole-pair preference and harmony, whereas contrasting hues can improve preference for the **foreground itself**, especially when lightness contrast is small.

## Problem and Motivation

Historical color theories and experiments use preference and harmony interchangeably or ask for the “best combination” without specifying what should look best. Contradictory findings about analogous versus complementary hues may therefore reflect different judgments rather than different laws of color. The authors use a Mozart–Stravinsky analogy: agreeing that one composition is more harmonious does not require preferring it.

## Mechanism and Study Design

All four experiments use the **same 48 participants, 24 male and 24 female**, screened with Dvorine plates; none had detected color deficiency. They completed **30 project tasks across eight sessions**, so these are four linked measurements, not four independent replications. The paper does not supply a full age or recruitment profile.

The stimulus set comprises **32 chromatic colors: eight hues × four saturation/lightness cuts** (saturated, light, muted, dark), calibrated on a CRT monitor. The appendix specifies measured CIE and Munsell coordinates; the cuts do not imply equal physical luminance across hues. Both orientations of every unequal pair produce **992 ordered combinations**, rather than 992 distinct unordered color pairs.

| Experiment | Stimuli and task | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| 1: Pair preference | Small centered square inside a larger colored square on neutral gray; all **992** combinations in random order | Continuous liking scale normalized to **−100 to +100** |
| 2: Pair harmony | Same nested squares and combinations, in a later session; explicit instruction to separate liking from “going together” | Continuous dissonant–harmonious rating |
| 3: Similarity | Equal-size squares side by side with a gap; both left/right orders; at least **one day** after harmony | Continuous different–similar rating |
| 4: Figural preference | **128 displays**, each showing the eight hues of one cut on one of 32 full-screen backgrounds; identical figure/background omitted | **1–9** liking rating for each visible foreground color |

Separate sessions reduce immediate question switching, but task order is fixed. Experiment 4 also changes layout, number of simultaneously visible colors, background extent, and rating format. Its contrast with pair preference cannot be attributed to instructions alone.

## Results / Admissions

### Pair preference is relational, not the sum of favorite colors

Pair preference peaks at matching hues and declines as hue difference increases. Figure/ground hue effects and their interaction are significant: **F(7,329)=8.32**, **F(7,329)=10.70**, and **F(49,2303)=25.42**, all **p<.001**. Complementary colors show no general preference advantage after correction; paint-complement preference versus neighboring hues is nonsignificant, **F(1,47)=1.53, p>.05**, although orange–blue is a reported exception, **F(1,47)=11.17, p<.008**.

Single-color liking explains only **21.7%** of variance in average pair preferences: **15%** from ground preference and **6.7%** additionally from figure preference. A Munsell model using cooler colors, smaller hue differences, and larger lightness differences explains **53.5%**; adding component preferences raises this to **62.9%**. The greater background contribution may reflect its greater area, not a general priority of backgrounds in design.

### Harmony correlates with preference but is not equivalent

Average pair harmony and preference correlate **r=.79**, with **62.3%** shared variance in the regression. Component preferences explain only **1.4%** of harmony variance versus **21.7%** of pair-preference variance. Harmony favors similar hues and relatively desaturated combinations; dark yellow/orange pairs can be harmonious yet disliked. Figure 8 makes these dissociations visible rather than merely reporting a high correlation.

A model combining harmony (**62.3%**), ground liking (**9.3%**), figure liking (**4.7%**), and lightness difference (**4.5%**) explains **80.8%** of pair-preference variance. These are incremental predictive contributions to stimulus-average ratings, not causal shares or individual-user accuracy.

Agreement with the group mean is greater for harmony (**mean r=.51**) than preference (**.36**), **t(47)=5.72, p<.001**. Individual preference–harmony correlations range from **−.03 to .75**. Formal color training has a quadratic association with preference for harmony, **F(1,47)=7.58, p<.01**: moderate training is associated with the strongest relationship, not a monotonic expert advantage. Figure 9 has small training subgroups (**7, 6, 20, 9, and 6**); the authors' education/experience explanation is speculative.

### Similarity is also a different judgment

Similarity correlates with harmony **r=.83**, and with pair preference **r=.55**. Similarity agreement with the group mean is **r=.75**, higher than harmony and preference, respectively **t(47)=8.84** and **14.39**, both **p<.001**. Lightness difference decreases similarity (**r=−.23, p<.001**) but slightly increases harmony (**r=.10, p<.01**). The opposite lightness relationships undermine the hypothesis that observers simply interpreted harmony as similarity.

Preference correlates more strongly with harmony than similarity within participants, **t(47)=8.24, p<.001**. Similarity adds no predictive variance when harmony is available; a preference model using similarity instead explains **71.3%**, below **80.8%** with harmony. A harmony–similarity correlation is not evidence that the constructs are interchangeable.

### Foreground liking benefits from contrast under particular conditions

Foreground preferences across backgrounds correlate **r=.87** with those colors' liking on neutral gray, compared with **r=.74** with pair preference; the participant-level difference is significant, **t(47)=4.64, p<.001**. A broader model explains **66.0%** of figural-preference variance: single-color liking **30.3%**, pair preference **18.7%**, pair similarity **12.0%** (less similarity favored), and signed saturation difference **5.0%** (more saturated figures on less saturated grounds favored).

After removing single-color and pair preferences, warmer foregrounds are preferred on cooler backgrounds and vice versa: residual hue interaction **F(49,2303)=7.69, p<.001**. The authors explicitly qualify this: the increasing-hue-contrast preference pattern is **primarily limited to similar-lightness pairs**. Lightness contrast generally benefits foreground liking; hue contrast is not an unconditional substitute.

A final pair-preference model using harmony, contextual figural liking, and ground liking explains **82.6%**, only modestly above **80.8%**. The paper admits that it establishes the pattern of judgments more strongly than their causes. Ecological associations, natural-image co-occurrence, perceptual fluency, discomfort near isoluminance, and simultaneous contrast are explanatory hypotheses, not experimentally isolated mechanisms here.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Define what should be liked before evaluating a palette.** A coherent pair and an appealing foreground can favor different hue relationships; neither is a usability or legibility measure.
2. **Keep aesthetic constructs separate even when correlated.** Harmony, similarity, and liking have distinguishable lightness and component-preference dependencies.
3. **Treat layout and context as part of the stimulus.** The foreground/background reversal effects are small for whole-pair liking, but contextual foreground liking cannot be reduced to a color's standalone appeal.
4. **Use group patterns as hypotheses, not prescriptions for every person.** The wide individual correlation range and training association warn against treating average harmony as universal preference.
5. **Do not infer accessibility from aesthetic contrast.** This sample excludes detected color deficiency and measures ratings, not reading or discrimination performance; contrast requirements belong to a separate evidence base.

## Questions and Limitations

- The stimulus family contains simple chromatic squares, not text, interfaces, meaningful objects, or full multi-color compositions. Evidence for other layouts and triples is described as preliminary, not reported as independent replications. Earlier studies corroborate some same-hue findings but use different definitions; no independent replication of this complete four-task design is established here.
- Repeated exposure and fixed task order may affect judgments. The musical analogy helps define harmony but cannot rule out every demand characteristic; the similarity dissociation rules out only a simple harmony-equals-similarity interpretation.
- Regressions search all combinations of ten Munsell predictors and choose models with the authors' **1-percentage-point** improvement rule. They describe this dataset; no held-out predictive validation or confidence intervals for explained variance are reported.
- Figure 8's **992** points include both orientations. Correlations over stimulus means are not independent participant effects. Group-agreement correlations also include each observer in the group mean.
- The text contains bibliographic inconsistencies: the introduction cites Granger **1995a–c** while the discussed studies and reference list are **1955a–c**, and cites Moon and Spencer **1994b** where the reference list gives **1944b**. The Experiment 3 text still labels left/right colors “figural” and “ground” in some analyses despite removing figure–ground layout; Figure 10 correctly labels left/right hue. These do not change the extracted judgment distinctions.
