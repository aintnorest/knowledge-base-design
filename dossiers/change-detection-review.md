# Change Detection — Study Notes

**Author**: Ronald A. Rensink.  
**Published**: Annual Review of Psychology **53 (2002), pp. 245–277**.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed narrative review; read the complete **33-page author-hosted production copy**, including three figures and references. The repeated **“30 Nov 2001 10:43”** typesetting stamp, source filenames, and crop marks identify a pre-publication production version rather than an independently verified final version of record. It already carries the 2002 journal pagination and copyright.

## What It Is

A conceptual and methodological review of first noticing visual change, including detecting its existence, locating it, and identifying what changed. It reports **no new participant experiment, systematic search, or pooled effect estimate**. Participant counts and study-level uncertainty are generally not supplied for the cited experiments; the numerical results below are review summaries, not newly measured effects.

The distinctive contribution beyond the later [attention and visual-memory survey](/dossiers/attention-visual-memory-visualization.md) is a careful separation of **motion, dynamic change, completed change, and difference**, and a map of how experimental task and change type alter the result.

## Distinctions as Ideas

- **Motion versus change:** motion describes variation at a location; change concerns an enduring structure whose properties transform. A motion signal can direct attention toward a change without being the same perceptual operation as recognizing that an object changed.
- **Dynamic change:** seeing the transformation itself as a visual event, potentially across a brief interruption.
- **Completed change:** recognizing that something changed while it was unavailable, without seeing the transformation. This may require linking a remembered and currently visible representation to the same entity.
- **Difference:** comparing two structures, such as side-by-side images, without necessarily treating them as one entity over time. This is not automatically equivalent to detecting a sequential change.

The proposed mechanism distinction between dynamic and completed change is **not established**. The review describes a phenomenological transition around **300 ms** of interstimulus interval and preliminary displacement evidence, but explicitly calls for behavioral corroboration. This is not an animation-duration rule.

## Evidence and Experimental Conditions

### Suppressing the transient can expose a missed change

Reviewed paradigms place changes during blank intervals, saccades, simulated scene shifts, blinks, occlusion, or film cuts. Brief unrelated “splats” can induce misses without covering the changed object; gradual changes over several seconds can be missed without any display interruption. Thus neither physical size nor uninterrupted optical availability guarantees awareness.

Figure 1 contrasts **one-shot** tests, primarily measured by accuracy, with repeated **flicker** tests, primarily measured by detection time. Repetition permits prolonged search but introduces eye movements and memory; a single presentation can separate addition from deletion, whereas repeated alternation mixes the two directions. Intentional search, divided attention, and incidental viewing test different conditions; incidental detection is generally worse, but intentional effort does not eliminate blindness.

### What changed matters, but task can reverse the ordering

- **Addition and deletion:** some deletions of unique items are detected more easily than additions. The asymmetry can disappear or reverse depending on how the initial and modified images will subsequently be used. The review cites Agostinelli and colleagues and Mondy and Coltheart; it supplies no universal magnitude or always-prefer-addition rule.
- **Property changes:** changes to a unique feature are generally easier than changes to a shared feature; swapping properties between items is especially difficult. A conjunction target changing two properties among items changing one is difficult, as is finding the one item that did not change.
- **Semantic identity:** substituting an object while retaining its broad visual appearance can go unnoticed. Meaningfulness and expertise guide selection but do not supply complete visual records.
- **Layout:** layout changes are easier than feature changes in some studies and harder in others. Grouping and encoding strategies are possible explanations, not an isolated causal finding. Removing an item confounds layout with existence change.

Comparing change types is intrinsically difficult because their magnitudes and visibility cannot simply be equated. The review warns against treating unlike stimulus manipulations as a clean channel ranking.

### Detection, localization, identification, and response are separate

Identification is generally harder than merely detecting change. Conscious reports, a feeling that change occurred, forced-choice guesses, and visuomotor adjustments need not reflect the same process. The review reports a feeling preceding visual experience by several seconds in approximately **one-third of observers** in cited work; counts and uncertainty are absent. Above-chance guessing of unseen changes is reported but disputed, while visuomotor responses can occur without conscious recognition.

For orientation change, cited array experiments suggest **4–5 simultaneously monitored items**. Search for change is summarized at about **100 ms/item**, versus about **20 ms/item** for comparison of already stored items. The author uses this contrast to argue for representational construction costs, not merely a comparison bottleneck. These are task-specific estimates, not a dashboard item capacity or a meta-analytic effect.

## Theory and Explicit Admissions

**Coherence theory** proposes volatile early “proto-objects,” stabilized through focused-attention feedback into coherent object representations that dissolve when attention leaves. Figure 2 depicts that feedback; Figure 3 adds a nonattentional gist/layout system guiding selection. These diagrams are **proposed models**, not measurements proving a single architecture.

The text argues strongly against an all-purpose detailed visual buffer and for sparse, task-specific representations. However, its Open Issues section qualifies an overly simple “not stored” reading:

- Misses can reflect **failure to access preserved information**, not only failure to maintain it; post-saccadic blanking can improve detection.
- Poor incidental detection cannot conclusively show fewer attended properties: fewer properties might instead be **compared**.
- Attention to an external object is **necessary but not sufficient** in the proposed account, because only some of its properties may be encoded. Whether all attended information is compared remains open.
- Whether dynamic change, completed change, motion, and difference use separate systems remains unresolved, as do the relation between explicit and implicit detection and which representations each can access.

These are tensions between the review's stronger explanatory claims and its explicit qualifications, not grounds for silently choosing the most categorical formulation. The later attention survey similarly preserves several possible storage, access, and comparison explanations.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Design for the return glance, not only the transition.** A person absent during an event must recognize a completed change; a transient alone cannot carry all necessary evidence.
2. **Evaluate additions, deletions, swaps, and value changes separately.** Their detection costs and asymmetries depend on what viewers must do with the display.
3. **Separate notice from localization and identification.** Knowing something changed does not establish knowing what changed or its significance.
4. **Treat persistent before/after evidence as a design hypothesis supported by the comparison problem**, not a tested intervention or guaranteed cure in this review.
5. **Preserve theoretical uncertainty.** Change blindness is a reliable family of phenomena across methods, not proof that memory is empty or one theory is uniquely correct.

## Questions and Limitations

- This historical narrative review covers evidence available around 2001, including conference abstracts and work in press. It cannot establish the current status of each mechanism, effect, or replication.
- Convergence across laboratories, stimuli, and paradigms supports change blindness broadly; no systematic replication audit, pooled miss rate, standardized effect sizes, or confidence intervals is supplied.
- No interface redesign, multi-agent supervision task, disability-specific comparison, or unattended-display deployment is tested here. Those applications are extrapolations.
