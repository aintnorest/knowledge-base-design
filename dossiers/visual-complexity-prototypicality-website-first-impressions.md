# Visual Complexity and Prototypicality — Study Notes

**Authors**: Alexandre N. Tuch, Eva E. Presslaber, Markus Stöcklin, Klaus Opwis, and Javier A. Bargas-Avila.  
**Published**: International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 70(11), 2012, pp. 794–811.  
**Status**: Read the Google-hosted author manuscript marked “Preprint submitted to Elsevier” and dated August 17, 2012; the source key and canonical reference identify the same-work published article.  

## What It Is

Two experiments examining how **visual complexity (VC)** and **prototypicality (PT)** relate to beauty judgments after extremely brief website exposure. Low complexity plus high prototypicality consistently produced the highest ratings. Both factors differentiated ratings even at **17 ms**, but familiarity with a category's visual model became more influential between **17 and 33–50 ms**.

This is a result about **passive visual first impressions of company homepages**, not a demonstration that people decide to leave a site within 17 ms, consciously recognize its content in that interval, or prefer minimalism during real tasks.

## Problem and Motivation

Earlier first-impression studies established that aggregate attractiveness judgments could be stable after brief exposures but did not adequately explain their visual basis. The authors test two candidate factors, rather than treat every attractive screenshot as evidence for an undefined “good design.”

Complexity is observer-relative: it concerns how complex a page appears, not a universal element-count threshold. Prototypicality means how representative a page is of its category, informed by learned expectations. It is not identical to recognizing the company or having visited that particular site.

## Mechanism as an Idea

The paper adapts Leder et al.'s aesthetic-processing model: **perceptual analysis** processes visual properties such as complexity, while **implicit memory integration** incorporates familiarity and prototypicality. Figure 1 includes feedback and continuous affective evaluation; it is not a strict one-way sequence with established millisecond boundaries.

The resulting hypothesis is that VC influences appeal earlier than PT. This receives partial temporal support in Study 2, but the experiments do not measure processing stages directly. A simpler interpretation is that some visual differences require more exposure to affect ratings.

The important interaction is a **joint constraint**, not two additive prescriptions. Typical organization helps little once the display is highly complex; low complexity alone does not rescue a page that looks unlike a company website. Figure 2 juxtaposes sparse atypical pages, conventional image-and-navigation compositions, and dense text-heavy pages. They are distinct real websites, not matched versions of one page with only one property changed.

## Results / Admissions

### Stimulus Construction and Measures

A pool of **464** popular company sites from seven business sectors, collected in **August 2010**, was reduced to **270** after exclusions for intro pages, shopping baskets, banners, archives, and language. **267 online raters** assessed VC and PT in sets of 30; average agreement was **ICC=.82 (SD=.09)** for VC and **.78 (SD=.06)** for PT. The authors selected **120 screenshots**, 20 in each of six VC×PT cells, aiming to vary the two dimensions independently. Screenshots were reduced from **1280×1024 to 1000×800** for presentation.

An additional **86-person** familiarity check found very low website familiarity (**M=1.04, SD=.04**) and low company/brand familiarity (**M=1.3, SD=.67**) on five-point scales; removing better-known brands did not change the main analytical conclusions. This controls recognition of particular sites, not learned category expectations.

Beauty was measured with an ugly–beautiful visual analog scale converted to **1–9**. A **1000-ms fixation**, timed screenshot, and **50-ms random black-and-white noise mask** preceded the rating. Masking limits continued visual persistence after offset; exposure duration is not response time. The same 60-Hz apparatus was used in both studies.

### Study 1: 50, 500, and 1000 ms

**59 participants**, **45 female**, mostly Basel psychology undergraduates; mean age **25.4 (SD=10.3; range 18–62)**; mean web experience **9.5 years**. None had visual/web-design education. Presentation duration was randomly assigned between participants; VC (low/medium/high) and PT (low/high) were within participants. A logging error left **119 analyzed stimuli**.

- VC: **F(1.8,99.9)=77.607, p<.001, partial η²=.581**; PT: **F(1,56)=241.365, p<.001, η²=.812**. These main effects must be read alongside a strong **VC×PT interaction**, **F(1.7,96.1)=85.273, p<.001, η²=.604**.
- High versus low PT differed much more at low and medium complexity (**d=1.96 and 1.79**) than at high complexity (**d=.24**). Under high PT, lower complexity was preferred; under low PT, **high VC slightly exceeded medium VC (d=.34)**. “Lower complexity is always better” misses that local reversal.
- At **50 ms**, high-PT beauty means were **5.52, 5.37, 4.59** for low/medium/high VC; low-PT means were nearly flat at **4.34, 4.37, 4.38**. At **1000 ms**, corresponding high-PT means were **5.58, 5.13, 4.14**, low-PT **3.91, 3.76, 3.95**.
- Neither VC×duration (**p=.099**) nor PT×duration (**p=.160**) was significant. The predicted temporal separation was **not supported in Study 1**.

### Study 2: 17, 33, and 50 ms

**82 participants**, **57 female**; mean age **27.3 (SD=9.9; range 16–63)**; mean web experience **9.8 years**, no design education. Removing medium complexity left **80 screenshots** in a 2×2 within-participant design. Duration groups contained **30, 24, and 28 participants**. Measured mean exposures were **16.67, 33.27, and 49.98 ms** (SDs **.47, .44, .44 ms**).

- VC: **F(1,79)=85.60, partial η²=.52**; PT: **F(1,79)=97.15, η²=.55**; VC×PT: **F(1,79)=30.85, η²=.28**; all significant at the paper's **p<.05** threshold.
- **PT×duration** was significant, **F(2,79)=8.83, η²=.18**; VC×duration was not, **F(2,79)=1.46, η²=.04**. Duration itself had no significant main effect.
- At **17 ms**, both factors were significant, but VC was stronger (**η²=.38**) than PT (**.19**). At **33 ms**, they were similar (**.65 and .66**); at **50 ms**, **.57 and .66**.
- At **17 ms**, the low-VC/high-PT cell averaged **4.89**, versus **4.31** for high-VC/low-PT. At **50 ms**, these means were **5.23 versus 4.01**. Figure 5 shows the strengthening PT separation, rather than a uniform rise in appeal with viewing time.
- PT's simple effect was larger under low VC (**d=.91**) than high VC (**d=.33**); VC's effect was larger under high PT (**d=.91**) than low PT (**d=.30**).

### A Replication That Exposes an Aggregation Artifact

The paper also reevaluates Tractinsky et al. (2006)'s claim that extremely beautiful or ugly pages receive faster judgments than middle-rated pages. Pooling participants reproduced the inverted-U relationship, but **2261 observations from 19 participants** were treated as independent in the 500-ms condition, inflating degrees of freedom. At one scale endpoint, **28%** of responses came from one person while seven people supplied none.

Participant-level analyses found the expected quadratic pattern in only **18–28%** of participants, with average additional explained variance only **2.0–3.3%**. Four showed a quadratic pattern in the opposite direction. Figure 6 contrasts three very different individual patterns. The broad claim that response latency measures aesthetic preference is therefore **not robust at the individual level**.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **Evaluate simplicity against the category model.** The strongest finding is the combination of low VC and high PT, not an absolute preference for empty pages or a mandate to copy competitors.
2. **Separate rapid appeal from useful interaction.** A masked screenshot can reveal an early impression, but cannot establish navigation performance, accessibility, conversion, or durable satisfaction.
3. **Learned convention is part of visual perception.** PT can influence ratings without prior familiarity with the particular brand, so novelty and recognizability need not be opposites.
4. **Preserve the unit of analysis.** The latency critique is reusable methodological evidence: pooled stimulus responses can imply a “typical user” pattern few people actually exhibit.

## Questions and Limitations

- Swiss, relatively young, experienced web users and company-homepage stimuli limit transfer across cultures, age groups, mobile interfaces, and other page categories. Category prototypes themselves can change.
- Real websites were selected, not surgically manipulated. Color, grouping, imagery, text, and spatial arrangement underlying VC/PT are neither isolated nor fully understood.
- The manuscript's **Table 2 appears to reverse high/low headings**: VC means **3.32, 4.19, 5.02** are labeled high/medium/low despite the high-complexity wording; PT means **3.78 and 4.99** are labeled high/low. Figure 2's examples and the main results instead align larger ratings with higher VC/PT. Treat this as a reporting inconsistency, not an inverted construct.
- The reported duration main effect in Study 1 pairs **F(2,56)=3.849** with **p=.222 and η²=.052**, an internally inconsistent statistical line. The paper interprets duration as nonsignificant; the dossier does not repair the value or derive a new result.
- The discussion's simple statement that low-PT/high-VC is worst is stronger than Study 1's cell pattern and small high-versus-medium reversal. The interaction is more informative than that slogan.
