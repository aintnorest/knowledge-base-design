# Letter Case and Text Legibility — Study Notes

**Authors**: Aries Arditi and Jianna Cho.  
**Published**: Vision Research **47(19), September 2007, pp. 2499–2505**; saved metadata identifies August 6, 2007 for publication of the final edited article.  
**Status**: Peer-reviewed journal research read as the saved **NIH author manuscript, PMC2016788 / NIHMS31268**, available in PMC from September 1, 2008, not the final publisher typesetting. Read the complete saved article, two tables, and all six figure images. Its publisher disclaimer identifies an accepted manuscript awaiting copyediting and proof review.  

## What It Is

A set of experiments comparing letter case through **size thresholds**, **rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) reading speed**, and **continuous-text reading speed**, using one unaltered font, **Arial**. Its key boundary is that uppercase's advantage appears when text is visually small relative to the reader's acuity. When text is well above that limit, the demonstrated speed advantage disappears.

The question is not whether capitals are more comfortable, familiar, attractive, or socially appropriate. It is whether larger uppercase letterforms at a fixed font-size setting improve visual identification and reading under size limitation.

## Problem and Motivation

Conventional explanations privilege lowercase word shapes because ascenders and descenders create distinctive contours. Figure 1 illustrates that argument, but is a conceptual diagram rather than its validation. A competing explanation is simpler: most uppercase letters are taller and wider than lowercase counterparts at the same point size.

Matching cap height to lowercase x-height does not fully equalize the stimuli either, because lowercase ascenders and descenders extend beyond x-height. The authors deliberately retain the practical typographic convention of fixed font size, accepting its uppercase size advantage. Thus the design tests the consequence of a commonly used setting, not a case effect independent of glyph dimensions.

## Mechanism as an Idea

### Participants and visual conditions

Table 1 lists **nine participants: five normally sighted and four with low vision**. Normal participants ranged from **20 to 72 years**, with chart acuities from **−0.2 to 0.2 logMAR**; low-vision participants were **72–83 years**, with **0.9–1.2 logMAR** acuity. The latter had clear ocular media and acuity loss associated with macular dysfunction: three age-related maculopathy diagnoses and one diabetic retinopathy diagnosis.

Subsets differ by task: **three normal and four low-vision readers** contributed size thresholds; **three normal and four low-vision readers** contributed small-text reading speeds; **two normal readers** contributed the subsequent large-text reading comparison. Normal recruits came from Lighthouse staff and volunteers, and low-vision readers from its clinical service. All were described as naïve to the experimental purpose.

Text was **black on white**, with letter and background luminances **3.6 and 129 cd/m²**. A long optically folded viewing distance and high-resolution letter renderings avoided low-resolution artifacts for normal readers' size thresholds; low-vision participants viewed directly at **100 cm**. Arial's relatively large x-height was selected partly to reduce upper/lower size differences. There was no contrast-polarity manipulation.

### Identification and reading are distinct measures

For thresholds, readers identified **five-letter words** or random **five-letter strings**. Words came from **2,110 frequent English five-letter words**, in all upper- or lowercase; random strings additionally had independently randomized letter case. Strings remained visible until response. A staircase decreased size after at least **four of five letters** were correctly identified in order, using **0.05 log-unit steps**, targeting approximately **68.6% letter accuracy**. Four runs per condition each ended at **15 reversals**; early observations before the second reversal were discarded. Table 2 records the balancing of case-block order, though random strings always preceded words.

For RSVP, single words of short expanded-MNREAD sentences appeared in sequence at a fixed central location; participants subsequently reported the sentence aloud. Rates sampled the error-producing region of the psychometric function; the fitted threshold was the rate supporting **50% correct**, not error-free fluent reading. Sentences had **56 characters**, treated as **9.33 standardized six-character words** for speed calculation.

Continuous reading used approximately **400-word**, ninth-grade-level passages, read aloud on screen, with speed computed from correctly read words divided by time. The main reading comparisons contrasted **all uppercase with ordinary mixed case**, not all lowercase with capitals.

The main text size was an **acuity reserve of approximately 2**: lowercase x-height subtended twice the chart-letter angle associated with the reader's acuity. That was near the low-vision readers' available reserve but unusually small for ordinary normal-vision reading. A later comparison used reserve **10**, with two normally sighted readers.

## Results / Admissions

- Lowercase size thresholds were approximately **0.1 log unit higher** than uppercase, about a **26% size difference** when converted as a ratio. This is a rounded summary, not a separately reported per-condition estimate with a confidence interval.
- Normal and low-vision thresholds differed: **F(1,6) = 58.82, p < .0003**. Words were easier to identify than random strings: **F(2,25) = 344.23, p < .0001**, as reported. This word-superiority result does not uniquely demonstrate recognition through word outlines.
- The case comparison favored uppercase: **F(2,25) = 12.19, p = .0002**. Random mixed-case thresholds were intermediate for normal readers but highest for low-vision readers. The authors note greater low-vision variability and a guessing-probability complication: mixed-case letter identities span **52 rather than 26 possibilities**.
- At reserve **2**, uppercase produced faster reading across the normal/low-vision and RSVP/continuous conditions: **F(1,20) = 5.530, p = .029**. Figures 4 and 5 show the corresponding group means and variability; the text gives no numerical percentage speed advantage or complete tabulated means. These should not be replaced by precise estimates read from the bars.
- At reserve **10**, Figure 6 shows no clear uppercase speed advantage in either method for the **two normal readers**. This was an afterthought comparison, not an adequately powered demonstration of equivalence or a large-text test with low-vision participants.

The authors recommend uppercase for size-limited reading when nominal point size is held fixed, while acknowledging its greater spatial footprint and lower aesthetic appeal. Preference was not measured in their experiments; historical preference figures in the introduction belong to Tinker's work, not this sample.

## Analyst Takeaways

1. **An uppercase micro-label is not automatically less legible.** When small text approaches acuity limits, capitals can buy visible letter size. This is a conditional visual-performance finding, not permission to make necessary text tiny.
2. **Evaluate case and size together.** The experiment deliberately does not equalize glyph area, cap height, and x-height; “same point size” is not “same visual information.”
3. **Do not generalize a threshold advantage to all reading.** Sustained comprehension, preference, visual search, unfamiliar abbreviations, and label interpretation remain separate questions.
4. **Make small-print evidence conditional on the reader.** An apparently normal interface size can still be near the acuity limit for a low-vision user. Conversely, a larger reserve can eliminate the observed case advantage.
5. **Do not claim a dark-UI result.** All experimental text was black on white; this source does not establish whether polarity strengthens, weakens, or reverses the case effect.

## Questions and Limitations

- Samples are very small and recruited through one organization. Vision group is confounded with age for most participants, and low vision is limited to the reported macular dysfunctions. No independent replication of these exact experiments is reported. The threshold direction is consistent with older distance-legibility findings discussed by the authors, but those do not replicate every reading-speed comparison.
- Random-case strings are not ordinary sentence case. Their case-sensitive identification task and different chance space complicate interpretation as a pure effect of outline shape.
- The RSVP result is a **50%-accuracy threshold**, whereas continuous reading is timed error-corrected oral reading. Treating their rates as interchangeable measures of ordinary comprehension would erase substantial method differences. The manuscript describes RSVP fit errors as characters but then defines the endpoint in word accuracy; that reporting ambiguity remains unresolved.
- **Internal inconsistencies**: recruitment prose describes **four normal participants**, but Table 1 lists **five** and **nine total**. The methods enumerate continuous reading as **Experiment 3**, whereas the later reading-speed section includes both methods under **Experiment 2**. Low-vision threshold results are referenced to **Figure 2** although their plot is **Figure 3**; small-text normal and low-vision reading results are referenced to **Figures 3 and 4** although their plots are **4 and 5**; the later large-text comparison refers to **Figure 5** although it is **Figure 6**. This dossier follows the displayed images and captions, not those cross-references.
- Some text errors may reflect the accepted-manuscript version rather than the final edited article. The large-text result supplies only **n = 2**, no formal equivalence analysis; absence of a visible advantage should not be inflated into proof of no case effect at all larger sizes.
- **Redistribution evidence**: the saved “Copyright and License information” panel contains only a link labeled **“PMC Copyright notice”**, with no article-specific Creative Commons or equivalent grant. The publisher disclaimer says **“all legal disclaimers that apply to the journal pertain.”** NIH deposit and free access are not permission to redistribute the saved bundle; use `archive/private/`.
