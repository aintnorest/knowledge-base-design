# Figure–Ground Organization

When two regions share a boundary, perception tends to assign that boundary to one region: the **figure**, experienced as a shaped, usually nearer surface. The **ground** appears to continue behind it. This border ownership differs from [perceptual grouping](/vault/perceptual-grouping-cues.md): elements can belong together without becoming a foreground figure.

## How It Works

Convexity, symmetry, smaller area, and surroundedness can favor figure assignment, but none is an unconditional rule. The surrounding composition changes their influence.

Other cues include a region's position below a horizontal border, a shape wider at its base, shading or texture suggesting a curved surface turning away, and similarities between an edge and its neighboring region. Motion and occlusion can reverse otherwise plausible interpretations: an advancing region can defeat the small-area bias, while lines terminating at a border can support interpretation of an occluded background.

Familiar shapes, attention, and perceptual expectations also affect ambiguous boundaries. Shape analysis is not merely a final step after an entirely bottom-up figure has already been selected.

## Evidence

The evidence comes from a **2012 narrative review**, not a pooled meta-analysis. Most underlying participant counts and standardized effects are not supplied in the dossier.

In **100-ms** displays, convex-as-figure judgments rose from about **57% with two regions** to **89% with eight regions**, but only when the alternating concave regions were homogeneous in color. This qualifies older roughly **90%** results from many-region, long-exposure displays. Brief two-region displays favored the symmetric region on approximately **three-quarters** of trials, yet symmetry could lose against convexity.

Head-tilt and inversion experiments suggested that the lower-region bias uses a predominantly retinal rather than environmental reference frame. Familiar-object configurations had greater figural influence when upright than inverted. Some convexity and symmetry segregation occurred without focal attention, while attention could bias ambiguous borders.

Neural recordings provide converging context evidence: about **half** of oriented monkey V2 neurons in one study were border-ownership selective, with context effects emerging around **60–70 ms** after stimulus onset. These findings constrain mechanisms; they do not establish a timing requirement for design.

## Applied Illustration: Changing Surface Polarity

A practitioner inspection of light and dark presentations illustrates how
darkening surfaces can erase distinctions that previously separated foreground
from background. Weakened overlay/background separation and floating elements
that blend into neighboring surfaces show why preserving literal content or
text contrast does not preserve perceived layering. The source recommends
surface differentiation and perceivable boundaries rather than assuming a
reversed shadow supplies equivalent depth.

These are **illustrated design observations, not a controlled figure–ground
study**. They demonstrate a reason to inspect the whole composition after a
presentation change; they do not establish a border-ownership effect size or
prove that every layer needs a border. Section membership is still a
[grouping question](/vault/perceptual-grouping-cues.md), distinct from perceiving
one shaped surface as nearer.

## Practical Use

As design inferences from the review:

- Audit foreground interpretation separately from membership: a well-grouped set may still compete with its surrounding surfaces.
- Inspect boundaries, occlusion, shading, and neighboring colors together rather than treating each surface in isolation.
- Check whether background shapes or familiar silhouettes unintentionally acquire the strongest shaped-surface interpretation.
- Test ambiguous arrangements in their actual composition and viewing conditions instead of relying on a single cue's supposed priority.
- After a polarity change, inspect whether foreground surfaces, overlays, and
  floating elements remain separate from their surrounds; preserve the intended
  relationship rather than mechanically reversing each color or shadow.

## Limitations

Cue effects depend on exposure, region count, color context, and the competing interpretation. Neither “all organization is preattentive” nor “attention is always required” fits the record. Ecological explanations such as gravitational stability are rationales, not directly proven causes. Feedback from larger-scale representations is a plausible neural account, not a settled implementation.

These findings explain perceptual organization, not the measured effectiveness of particular layouts or a universal recipe for visual emphasis.

The applied polarity examples extend the research as a design illustration,
not a replication. Their source reports no controlled layering comparison,
usability-test sample size, or quantitative outcome for those inspections.
