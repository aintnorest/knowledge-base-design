# Juxtaposability for Comparison

Juxtaposability is the ability to view related information together. It differs from simple availability: two representations can both be accessible yet still require repeated switching to compare them. The concept applies to paper, diagrams, notations, and screens, not only software navigation.

## How It Works

When comparison requires successive views, people must retrieve one item, retain its relevant details, move to another, and mentally combine them. A persistent shared space can make those relations inspectable instead. Comparison becomes movement among visible evidence rather than reconstruction of absent evidence.

Merely placing items beside one another is insufficient. Their arrangement must expose the relation the task requires. Alignment by a shared attribute can support checking differences or alternatives; a different question may require a different organization. Comparative richness means retaining useful evidence, not putting every available datum in one place.

This complements [recognition over recall](/vault/recognition-over-recall.md) and reduces reliance on [working-memory capacity](/vault/working-memory-capacity-limits.md), but the sources here do not quantify that reduction.

## Evidence and Tradeoffs

A cognitive-dimensions tutorial distinguishes visibility from juxtaposability and describes repeated revisiting, memory dependence, or external copying when related components cannot be seen together. Its examples make the structural problem explicit, but the framework is a discussion vocabulary rather than a validated outcome scale.

One illustrative editing comparison reported **508.3, 193.6, and 63.3 seconds** to transcribe the same precomputed change in three representations—roughly **8:1** between extremes. Distributing a representation across more windows reduced local rearrangement while trading away visibility and juxtaposition. The authors call this a **“straw-test”**: the tutorial supplies only an experienced-user description, no participant sample beyond it, repeated-trial distribution, or inferential statistics. These times do not isolate a causal effect of juxtaposability.

A separate design essay demonstrates catalog and schedule representations that expose comparisons through spatial arrangement. The worked alternatives show which relationships become inspectable. They report **no recruited participants, task-success measures, time distributions, error rates, or inferential statistics** for those redesigns. Their contribution is inspectable design rationale, not proof that spatial comparison always outperforms sequential access.

## Practical Use

- Identify the facts people need to compare before choosing a representation or navigation sequence.
- Keep related evidence available together for consistency checks, revision, and choosing among alternatives.
- Align comparable attributes around the actual question; preserve secondary relationships when they remain relevant.
- When restructuring reduces editing effort but fragments the view, explicitly assess the resulting comparison burden using [cognitive-dimensions tradeoffs](/vault/cognitive-dimensions-tradeoffs.md).
- Assess understanding as well as navigation effort: fewer transitions are useful when the required comparison becomes clearer.

## Limitations

Juxtaposition consumes space and may increase verbosity, layout work, or visual-search demands. A representation helpful for one question can obscure another. Viewing together does not eliminate interpretation, literacy, or access barriers; the essay's visual examples do not develop nonvisual alternatives.

The strongest evidence in these dossiers is conceptual and demonstrative. Neither supplies a replicated effect size for side-by-side comparison or a universal layout optimum. Historical editing results concern particular representations and a particular change, not a permanent ranking of media or products.
