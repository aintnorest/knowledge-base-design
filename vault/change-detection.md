# Change Detection and Change Blindness

A visible change need not become a noticed change. People can miss large changes
when a blink, eye movement, interruption, or competing transient prevents the
change from directing attention. Gradual changes can also be missed without an
interruption. Looking toward an object does not guarantee that its relevant
property was encoded, remembered, and compared.

## A Transition Is Not a Completed Change

**Dynamic change detection** means seeing a transformation happen. **Completed
change detection** means recognizing that something changed while it was not
being watched. Someone returning to an unattended display faces the second
problem even if a transition was conspicuous to a continuous viewer.

Comparing side-by-side representations is another task: detecting a difference
between two structures does not necessarily involve recognizing the same entity
over time. Persistent old/new information can reduce dependence on remembered
detail, but the reviews do not supply a controlled interface intervention showing
that this guarantees discovery or comprehension.

The conceptual distinctions are useful without assuming separate brain systems.
The historical review explicitly leaves their mechanistic separation unresolved;
its approximately **300 ms** phenomenological boundary is not an interface timing
requirement.

## Change Type and Task Alter What Is Easy

- **Additions and deletions:** some deletions of unique items are easier to detect
  than additions, but subsequent use of the two images can eliminate or reverse
  that advantage. Neither direction is inherently a reliable notification.
- **Value changes and swaps:** changing a unique property is generally easier
  than switching properties between items. Seeing an object does not imply that
  its color, identity, position, and relationships are all available for comparison.
- **Layout changes:** grouping and the viewer's encoding strategy can change
  whether layout or individual-feature changes are easier. Removing an object
  changes both existence and layout, so it does not isolate either mechanism.
- **Goals and expectations:** intentional search generally beats incidental
  noticing, while expertise and meaning guide selection. Even intentional search
  can fail; attention to an object is not sufficient for every property change.

## Evidence and What It Does Not Prove

The historical review brings together blank-gap, eye-movement, blink, occlusion,
film-cut, distractor, and gradual-change paradigms, including real-world person
substitution. One-shot studies primarily measure accuracy; repeated flicker
studies primarily measure search time and mix addition with deletion directions.
Convergence supports change blindness across methods, not one universal miss rate.

The later attention/visual-memory survey reports a cited film experiment in which
nearly **two-thirds** missed replacement of the principal actor. It also separates
**inattentional blindness**—missing an unexpected event while engaged elsewhere—from
failure to compare changed states. Neither phenomenon is simply poor optical
visibility, and neither supplies a universal notification-failure percentage.

Change blindness does **not** prove that no prior representation exists. Missing
encoding, loss of information, inaccessible storage, incompatible representations,
and failure to compare can contribute. Rensink argues for sparse, task-dependent
representations and proposes coherence theory, but explicitly admits unresolved
access and comparison alternatives. The later survey preserves that uncertainty.

## Practical Use

- Evaluate changes **between glances**, not only while the viewer watches them
  happen. Include engaged-elsewhere and unexpected-change conditions.
- Keep consequential changed state available for deliberate inspection; consider
  explicit change summaries or comparable old/new evidence when memory would
  otherwise carry the comparison. Evaluate these as design hypotheses.
- Test addition, deletion, substitution, value change, and reordering separately
  where each can affect the person's task; do not transfer one asymmetry blindly.
- Measure **notice, localization, identification, and understanding** separately.
  A report that something changed does not establish what changed or its meaning.
- Do not rely on a stronger transient alone to guarantee discovery. Relate event
  signaling to [motion cues and distraction](/vault/motion-cues-attention-and-distraction.md)
  and conditional [preattentive features](/vault/preattentive-features.md).

## Limitations

These are narrative reviews rather than pooled intervention estimates. The older
review reflects evidence available around 2001 and includes preliminary abstracts;
it does not establish the present status of each proposed mechanism. The later
survey qualifies rather than uniquely resolves the memory theories. Neither
validates a particular notification design, accessibility accommodation, number
of simultaneously monitorable interface items, or supervision workflow.
