# Choice Architecture Effects

The presentation of alternatives is part of the decision, not a neutral wrapper.
Preselection, endorsement, position, comparison burden, and conspicuous status
cues can change which option people consider or choose without changing the
nominal set of alternatives. **Changed choice is not necessarily better choice**:
uptake, informed preference, accuracy, and benefit require different evidence.

## Separate the Interventions

| Intervention | What changes | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| **Default or preselection** | An option applies unless actively rejected. | Staying means informed agreement, or the effect is only reduced effort. |
| **Recommendation label** | An option receives an endorsement or attributed source. | A label increases uptake, establishes quality, or acts like a default. |
| **Order and initial visibility** | Some alternatives are encountered earlier or require disclosure. | Available options receive equal consideration, or gaze proves comprehension. |
| **Number and composition of options** | The search space and relationships among alternatives change. | Fewer options always help, or there is one optimal count. |
| **Salience/status cue** | One alternative is marked as awaiting attention. | Attracting a first click establishes real urgency or improved experience. |

These interventions can coexist, but combining them makes attribution harder.
The broad nudge synthesis excluded combined techniques; its estimates do not
validate a package of preselection, prominent placement, and endorsement.

## Defaults: A Strong Average with Large Exceptions

A meta-analysis of **58 datasets from 55 studies, reported N = 73,675**, compared
opt-out with opt-in choices. Its mean was **d = .68, 95% CI [.53, .83]**, or
**.63 [.47, .80]** after accounting for nesting. Heterogeneity was approximately
**I² = 98%**: **46** observations were significantly positive, **10** nonsignificant,
and **2** significantly negative. The mean establishes plausible influence, not a
forecast for a particular consent flow or configuration.

Three proposed channels are **endorsement** by the choice architect, **ease** of
remaining selected, and **endowment/status quo** as a reference point. Coder-inferred
endorsement predicted larger effects; the endowment association weakened under
robust estimation, and ease was not a detected moderator. These are study-level
associations, **not causal mediation**. No detected ease moderator does not show
that switching friction is irrelevant: effort variation and its coding were limited.
The authors recommend local testing rather than assuming universal effectiveness.

## Recommendation Labels: Endorsement Is Conditional

Two hypothetical smartphone meal-choice studies found no dependable general
benefit from a generic recommendation banner. The first, reported **N = 517**,
found no recommendation main effect (**p = .477**) or vertical-position main
effect (**p = .473**), but a recommendation-by-price interaction (**p < .01**).
That interaction **did not recur** in a second study, reported **N = 916**, where
price and prior preference were more consequential. The follow-up changed meals,
preference elicitation, labels, and the response measure from binary selection
to graded inclination; it was not an exact replication. Null significance tests
are not equivalence tests or evidence that recommendation labels are harmless.

A generic endorsement also differs from **who is said to recommend**. In numeric
judgment experiments, identical advice labeled algorithmic received greater
weight than advice labeled human: **.45 versus .30, d = .42**, in one weight-
estimation task. This was before performance feedback, with effects qualified by
comparison against oneself and by participant experience. It does not establish
universally calibrated trust or better accuracy from an algorithmic label. See
[appropriate reliance on automation](/vault/appropriate-reliance-on-automation.md)
for the distinction between advice uptake and capability-sensitive reliance.
The generic meal banner did not test named human or algorithmic authority.

## Order and Visibility: Exposure Is Unequal

A laboratory web-questionnaire study recruited **120 people**, with roughly
**106 usable recordings per experiment**. Reversing response order produced
more attention to early alternatives, but significant primacy in choice was not
universal: only the **12-option** child-quality item among four initial questions
had a significant first-half choice share (**60%, p = .04**).

When lists initially exposed five of ten substantive options, **43% and 41%** of
respondents in two questions never revealed the lower half. First-half selection
was **63%/78%**, versus **52%/69%** with all options visible and **52%/53%** with
none initially visible. Even people who revealed the rest allocated **64%/60%**
of gaze time to the initially visible half. The finding concerns **asymmetric
initial exposure**, not a universal ranking of control types. Gaze–choice
associations do not isolate whether looking changes preference or preference
directs looking. The null position effect in the two-meal study also prevents
turning early-position advantage into a universal rule.

## Choice Overload: Diagnose the Burden, Not a Count

A conceptual review and meta-analysis of **99 observations from 53 published
studies**, reported **N = 7,202**, associated greater overload with four conditions:

- **Complex option relationships:** hard-to-align attributes or no dominant option.
- **Difficult decision tasks:** many attributes, time pressure, or justification.
- **Uncertain preferences:** limited expertise or no articulated ideal.
- **Decision goals that make selection effort salient:** choosing an item is
  different from browsing or choosing an assortment.

Its complete-set unconditional average was **nonsignificant**; a subset excluding
assortment-choice observations yielded **b = .17, p < .001**. Thus liking a broad
catalog and choosing successfully from it are different outcomes. Common
experimental comparisons of **6 versus 24 options** are not a threshold: neither
linear nor quadratic option-count predictors were significant. There is **no
established optimal count**. Helping people compare attributes or clarify
preferences is an application of the framework, not a tested head-to-head remedy.
The original estimates remain qualified by an identified but unread corrigendum.

## Badges: Redirected Action Is Not Justified Priority

In a **1,009-person** static-screen first-click experiment, exactly one of fifteen
icons carried a status badge. All fifteen within-study comparisons favored the
badged alternative, with absolute increases of **9.1–41.5 percentage points**
calculated from rounded proportions. The comparator was that same alternative
without a badge **while a competitor carried one**, not a badge-free screen.

The study observed choice redirection, not attention, urgency, understanding,
satisfaction, wellbeing, or sustained use. Salience and perceived urgency are
proposed explanations, not independently measured mechanisms. Fifteen comparisons
sharing participants are not fifteen independent replications; assignment was
not explicitly documented as random, and fixed positions limit causal attribution.

## Broad Nudge Effectiveness Remains Disputed

Mertens and colleagues' **corrected** synthesis of **447 effects from 212
publications** reports **d = .43, 95% CI [.38, .48]**. Yet **I² = 99.52%** and the
**95% prediction interval [−.36, 1.22]** admit wide variation and negative effects.
Corrected decision-structure, information, and assistance means were **.54, .34,
and .28**; the default estimate was **.62**. These are not publication-bias-adjusted
estimates or randomized head-to-head rankings. Their own selection scenarios
reduced a simplified-model mean to **.31** under moderate assumed bias and **.08**
under severe assumed bias.

Maier and colleagues' reanalysis of the corrected data adjusts publication bias
and obtains **d = .04, 95% credible interval [.00, .14], BF01 = .95** when using all
estimates without clustering. **BF01 near one is inconclusive, not proof of a
zero mean.** Retaining only the most precise estimate gives **d = .11 [.00, .24],
BF01 = .31**, favoring an effect to a limited degree. Information and assistance
show stronger evidence against average effects; structure remains inconclusive
or modestly positive, depending on the variant.

The tradeoff is unresolved: one adjustment ignores dependence, while the other
discards outcomes and changes representativeness. Adjustment depends on model
assumptions, but leave-one-out robustness cannot answer systematic publication
bias. Both accounts belong beside the headline. Heterogeneity persists, so neither
a positive unadjusted mean nor an unsupported adjusted mean settles whether a
particular intervention helps a particular population.

## Practical Use

- Specify the intervention and the user's decision goal before choosing a visual
  treatment. Keep no-default, no-recommendation, or appropriately exposure-matched
  controls; do not mistake existing preference for a presentation effect.
- Test the actual options, population, stakes, and device. Measure understanding,
  preference fit, refusal/correction, and consequences alongside selection rates.
  Report uncertainty and adverse effects, not only intended-direction uplift.
- Where alternatives are unordered, counterbalance order when estimating
  preferences; preserve meaningful scale order. Examine whether hidden options
  are actually encountered rather than merely technically available.
- Diagnose complexity, preference uncertainty, and task demands before removing
  options. Do not adopt an experimental assortment count as a design standard.
- **Ethical boundary:** support the person's informed decision rather than merely
  steering toward an organizational target. State the rationale for a default or
  endorsement, keep alternatives discoverable and easy to choose, and make status
  cues truthful and proportionate to actual relevance. Nominal freedom to opt out
  is insufficient if presentation conceals influence or burdens refusal; review
  [deceptive-pattern attributes](/vault/deceptive-pattern-attributes.md).
- **Extrapolation to AI-agent interfaces:** recommendations, preselected approvals,
  and prominent pending-work cues may steer supervisory choices without improving
  review. Test error detection and decision quality, and preserve inspectable,
  dismissible assistance; [mixed-initiative interaction](/vault/mixed-initiative-interaction.md)
  addresses when assistance should act, ask, or wait. These sources do not validate
  such effects in software-agent supervision.

## Limitations

The meta-analyses cover different historical evidence sets and outcomes, often
outside software. Their standardized effects are not interchangeable with click
percentage-point differences or a product's expected uplift. Uptake and intended-
direction behavior are not independent measures of welfare, informed consent,
or fairness. Study-level moderator associations do not establish mechanisms.

The overload review's substantive corrigendum was inaccessible, so its numerical
results describe the original article only. The order study has unresolved sample
and response-aggregation reporting inconsistencies; the meal studies have
participant/exclusion ambiguities and no precise label-versus-control equivalence
bounds. Short hypothetical choices and one-click tasks do not establish long-term
habituation, purchases, professional decisions, or cross-population transfer.
