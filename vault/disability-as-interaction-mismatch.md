# Disability as Interaction Mismatch

An interaction excludes someone when its demands do not fit the capabilities and resources available to that person in that situation. Treating disability as an interaction mismatch shifts the design question from “What is wrong with this user?” to “Which demand creates the barrier, and what can change?” The encounter includes products, environments, social arrangements, and relationships—not only an individual's bodily condition.

This is a design lens, not a complete account of disability. It directs attention toward changeable barriers without erasing embodied impairments, chronic consequences, or structural discrimination.

## The Persona Spectrum

A persona spectrum connects a shared interaction constraint across **permanent, temporary, and situational** conditions. Limited one-handed interaction, for example, can arise from having one arm, an arm injury, or holding an infant. Reduced access to spoken information can arise from deafness, an ear infection, or a noisy working environment.

The reusable relationship is a common interaction limitation, not equivalent identities, experiences, or severity. A sensory/motor matrix is also not a comprehensive disability taxonomy: learning, memory, social resources, language, culture, and emotional context can produce mismatches beyond its categories. Relationship networks can reveal exclusion affecting coworkers, family, friends, and other participants as well as the focal individual.

## Solve for One, Extend to Many

Start with a concrete exclusion and learn with the person who experiences it. Then investigate where the same constraint appears elsewhere and whether the proposed solution transfers. This gives depth to problem discovery without assuming that one participant represents a population.

The method recommends learning from actual adaptations, motivations, frustrations, and workarounds. Blindfolds or earplugs alone are misleading substitutes: a brief simulation does not reproduce learned adaptations or emotional context. A situational analogy must not replace participation by people living with permanent disabilities.

Extension is a **transfer hypothesis**, not a guaranteed benefit. Similar motor or sensory constraints can coexist with different motivations, safety requirements, attention, and assistive technologies. A solution that could benefit another context still needs evaluation there.

## Evidence and Its Limits

The cited guide is experience-informed design-method guidance, not an intervention experiment. It attributes its principles to **hundreds of hours of research and conversations**, but supplies no participant count, recruitment details, interview protocol, comparison conditions, effect sizes, or longitudinal outcome measures. Wider-utility examples, such as captions in noisy environments or contrast in sunlight, illustrate possible transfer rather than demonstrate comparative gains.

Its one-arm population graphic gives **26K permanent**, **13M temporary**, and **8M situational**, totaling **21M+**. The permanent figure is **annual incidence**; the time bases of the other figures are unspecified. The supporting institutions are named without reproducible publication references, measurement years, uncertainty, or overlap handling. The graphic therefore does not establish 21 million distinct simultaneous beneficiaries or a current market-size estimate. It motivates investigating reach beyond the initial exclusion.

## Practical Use

- Describe the interaction demand that blocks participation before prescribing a solution.
- Involve excluded people early and learn from their expertise rather than substituting imagined personas or simulations.
- Map permanent, temporary, and situational constraints to discover candidate transfers, then test the shared constraint in each relevant context.
- Consider participants' relationships as well as their individual abilities when identifying barriers.
- Keep inclusive design, accessibility conformance, and usability separate. The method can uncover exclusions; standards such as [minimum target size](/vault/minimum-target-size.md) establish particular boundaries; neither alone demonstrates successful participation.

## Limitations

The method does not promise one universal design for everyone or certify compliance with accessibility standards. Its claimed improvements in access, friction, and emotional outcomes are goals requiring measurement, not demonstrated causal effects in the guide. “Solve for one” leaves open whose experiences are represented and whose adaptations differ. Extending a solution must not dilute the original participant's needs or collapse permanent disability into temporary inconvenience. The cited population claims belong to historical guidance and are not automatically refreshed estimates.
