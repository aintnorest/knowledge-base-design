# Color Combination Preference and Harmony

A color combination can look harmonious without being liked, and its foreground
can be appealing without the whole pair being preferred. “Good colors” is an
underspecified goal. Distinguish **pair preference** (liking the whole),
**harmony** (colors going together), **similarity** (perceptual resemblance), and
**foreground preference** (liking a color against its particular background).
These responses are related, not interchangeable.

## Evidence for Distinct Judgments

Four linked experiments used the **same 48 participants**, with no detected
color-vision deficiency. Pair preference and harmony were judged for **992 ordered
combinations** of **32 chromatic colors**, presented as nested squares. Similarity
used side-by-side squares; foreground preference used multiple colored figures
on larger backgrounds. These are linked measurements, not four independent
replications.

Whole-pair preference and harmony generally favored similar hues. Complementary
hues had no general pair-preference advantage, although orange–blue was an
exception. Component-color preferences explained **21.7%** of average pair
preference variance but only **1.4%** of harmony variance. Liking the individual
colors therefore contributes to liking the combination, but explains little of
whether they seem to belong together.

Average harmony and pair preference correlated **r=.79**, yet some dark
yellow/orange combinations were harmonious and disliked. Individual
preference–harmony correlations ranged from **−.03 to .75**. Group agreement
about harmony does not mean that every observer prefers harmonious pairs.

Similarity correlated with harmony at **r=.83**, but lightness difference had
opposite relationships with the two: **r=−.23** for similarity and **r=.10** for
harmony. Greater lightness difference also contributed to pair preference. A
model combining harmony, component preferences, and lightness difference
explained **80.8%** of stimulus-average pair-preference variance; these are
predictive associations, not causal shares or individual-user accuracy.

## Foreground Appeal Can Favor Contrast

Foreground preference depended more strongly on the color's standalone appeal
than whole-pair preference did. Average foreground liking across backgrounds
correlated **r=.87** with liking on neutral gray, versus **r=.74** with whole-pair
preference. After accounting for standalone and pair liking, contrasting warm
and cool hues benefited foreground appeal, unlike the similar-hue tendency for
whole pairs.

That hue-contrast pattern was **primarily limited to similar-lightness pairs**.
Lightness contrast generally benefited foreground liking; opposing hues are not
an unconditional substitute. The foreground experiment also changed layout,
background extent, number of visible colors, and rating format, so the reversal
cannot be attributed solely to asking a different question.

## Practical Use

- Specify whether the intended outcome is an appealing composition, harmonious
  relationships, perceived similarity, or an appealing focal color.
- Measure those judgments separately rather than treating harmony as a proxy
  for preference or similarity.
- Evaluate the foreground on its actual background. Standalone swatches omit
  relationships that contribute to contextual appeal.
- Treat similar-hue pair preference and contrast-based foreground appeal as
  hypotheses to evaluate with the intended audience, not universal palette laws.
- Assess readability and discrimination separately through
  [Color Contrast Minimum](/vault/color-contrast-minimum.md) and
  [Color Coding for Status](/vault/color-coding-for-status.md). Aesthetic liking
  does not certify either.

## Limitations

The evidence concerns simple chromatic squares, not text, meaningful objects,
complete interfaces, or multi-color compositions. All tasks involved one sample,
with fixed task order and repeated exposure; no independent replication of the
complete design is established in the dossier. The sample excluded detected
color-vision deficiency and measured ratings, not reading or task performance.

The regressions describe stimulus-average ratings from the fitted dataset,
without held-out predictive validation or confidence intervals for explained
variance. Both orientations of each unequal pair contribute to the ordered
stimulus set. Proposed explanations involving associations, fluency, or
simultaneous contrast were not independently manipulated. These results separate
judgments more securely than they establish their causes.
