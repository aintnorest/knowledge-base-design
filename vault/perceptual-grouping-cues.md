# Perceptual Grouping Cues

Perceptual grouping determines which visible elements belong together. It organizes relationships among marks rather than simply registering each mark independently. A group need not become a foreground object: assigning a boundary to a shaped surface is the separate problem of [figure–ground organization](/vault/figure-ground-organization.md).

## How It Works

Several cues can reinforce or compete with one another:

- **Proximity and similarity** favor elements that are relatively near or share properties such as color, size, or orientation.
- **Common region and connectedness** favor elements inside one boundary or joined by a line or shared border.
- **Common fate** favors elements moving together; shared changes in another feature can also support membership.
- **Good continuation, parallelism, symmetry, and closure** favor compatible contours and bounded shapes.

Good continuation also helps integrate fragmented contours. Smoothly aligned edge fragments can form a visible path through clutter even without an uninterrupted line. Occlusion makes surface and depth interpretation important: **amodal completion** supports a unified shape behind an obstruction without a visible missing border, whereas **modal completion** supplies an illusory visible border. Choosing which fragments belong together is not the same as estimating the missing contour's shape.

## Evidence

A narrative review brings together psychophysics, image statistics, discrimination tasks, and neural evidence; it has no pooled sample or common effect size. Most reviewed studies' participant counts are not supplied in the dossier.

In regular dot lattices presented for **300 ms**, competing organizations depended on distance ratios. An orientation was almost never reported when its competing distance exceeded the shortest by roughly **1.5×**. This is a result about ambiguous lattices, not a universal spacing rule. Proximity and contrast similarity showed additive contributions in log-odds space in some lattice tasks; other contour tasks showed nonadditive interactions.

In labeled natural-edge data, proximity reduced grouping uncertainty by roughly **75%**, versus roughly **10% each** for continuation and similarity. These are dataset-specific information measures, not shares of attention or rankings for design layouts. Controlled contour displays also showed that aligned orientations reveal paths that disappear when orientations are randomized while spacing is held constant.

## Practical Use

The following are design inferences, not evaluated layout interventions:

- Make spacing, appearance, enclosure, and connection agree when they represent the same relationship.
- Compare within-group distances with competing relationships rather than choosing an isolated gap size.
- Check whether boxes, connecting lines, or accidental smooth contours imply membership that the content does not support.
- Inspect the surrounding surfaces and occluders when fragmented shapes should read as one object.

## Limitations

There is no universal cue-strength ordering. Regular lattices, contour detection, and real compositions involve different tasks and measurements. Closure's independent detection advantage shrinks under better continuation controls; the claim that uniform connectedness always supplies the earliest units is disputed. Temporal synchrony as an independent grouping cue remains unclear and should not be confused with a universal neural binding mechanism.

Organization can reflect depth, experience, and attention as well as retinal relations. The underlying review describes the field in **2012**, not a current exhaustive consensus.
