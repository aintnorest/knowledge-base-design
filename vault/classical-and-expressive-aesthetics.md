# Classical and Expressive Aesthetics

Visual aesthetics is not a single judgment of decoration. People can appreciate
an interface for its clarity and order, its creativity and originality, or both.
**Classical aesthetics** names the orderly, clear dimension; **expressive
aesthetics** names the creative, original dimension. They are related perceptions,
not opposite ends of a simplicity–novelty slider.

A broader model distinguishes **simplicity, diversity, colorfulness, and
craftsmanship** beneath a general visual-aesthetics factor. These are evaluated
qualities: appreciated organization, valued variety, pleasing color composition,
and coherent, careful execution. Colorfulness here is not pixel saturation, and
diversity is not a command to maximize complexity. The dimensions describe what
people appreciate, not rules specifying how much of a feature to add.

## Evidence for Distinguishable Perceptions

The classical–expressive model developed across samples of **125, 212, 145, and
384 participants**. The final sample was split into **192 initial-validation and
192 holdout participants**. The refined two-factor model fit the holdout data
with **RMSEA=.052 and CFI=.982**, whereas a single-factor alternative fit poorly
(**RMSEA=.156 and CFI=.836**). Internal consistency was **α=.85/.86** for classical
and **α=.87/.86** for expressive aesthetics in the two halves.

The dimensions correlate positively, but the statistics are not interchangeable:
the aesthetics-only latent-factor correlation was approximately **.61**, while
scale intercorrelations were **.69/.63**. Stronger perceived-usability correlations
for classical aesthetics partly reflect visible clarity and measurement overlap;
see the [Aesthetic–Usability Effect](/vault/aesthetic-usability-effect.md).

The four-facet model was refined with **506 volunteers** and independently
confirmed within the same research program with **512 volunteers evaluating
42 sites**. Confirmation fit was **RMSEA=.064 and CFI=.945**; facet internal
consistency ranged from **α=.85 to .89**, with **α=.94** for the total. The validity
analysis reused those **512 people**, rather than adding an independent sample.
Simplicity correlated with classical aesthetics **r=.82**, and diversity with
expressive aesthetics **r=.80**. This connects the models without making them
identical or establishing that the earlier model was wholly wrong.

## Facets Can Reveal What a Total Hides

In a randomized color-only comparison with **78 participants**, the colorfulness
facet changed by **d=.57**, while the total's **d=.19** was nonsignificant. The
colorfulness result used a **one-sided p<.01** threshold. A separate bundled
appearance comparison with **375 participants** changed all facets and the total
(**d=1.22** for the total), but altered several visual properties together. These
experiments support sensitivity to appearance changes, not independent causal
weights for each facet or a universal color recipe.

## Practical Use

- Specify which aesthetic perception a study or design critique addresses rather
  than treating beauty, clarity, and originality as interchangeable.
- Inspect relevant facets alongside an overall score; a targeted change may be
  visible in one dimension without substantially moving the total.
- Keep aesthetic appraisal separate from content quality, trust, perceived
  usability, and measured task performance.
- Use validated measurement to assess a design hypothesis, not as a checklist
  instructing designers to maximize every physical feature.

## Limitations

Factor fit and internal consistency support measurement, not causal preference
rules, temporal stability, or better performance. The classical–expressive
holdout came from the same recruiting ecosystem; the four-facet samples shared
a German cultural background. Neither program establishes cross-cultural
invariance, test–retest stability, mobile transfer, or actual returning behavior.

Perceptions remain partly overlapping. The two-factor validation allowed a
clear-design indicator to load on usability as well as classical aesthetics;
the four-facet total correlated with perceived usability **r=.64** and content
quality **r=.62**. Bundled real-site quality and shared rating methods can help
produce such associations. Some diversity wording is also too abstract to
diagnose a specific design change. A reliable score is not a self-sufficient
design explanation.
