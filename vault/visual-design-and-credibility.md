# Visual Design and Credibility

People can use visual presentation when judging whether a website is believable. Layout, typography, white space, images, and color can signal professional care or apparent sloppiness. These judgments concern **perceived credibility**, not the truth of the information or the trustworthiness of its author.

## Prominence–Interpretation

A credibility cue must pass two distinct gates:

1. **Prominence**: the person encounters and notices it.
2. **Interpretation**: the person judges what it implies in this context.

A favorable interpretation of an assurance is irrelevant if the assurance goes unnoticed. Conversely, an immediately visible cue can enter a judgment without providing substantive evidence. The theory's expression “Prominence × Interpretation = Credibility Impact” is a conceptual dependency, not a fitted equation or a method for calculating trust.

People may cycle through multiple observations until satisfied or constrained by time. Interpretation also depends on the domain: broad coverage, narrow focus, restrained advertising, reputation, or a functional test can mean different things in different tasks.

## Evidence

A live-site comparison study had **2684 participants** judge two assigned websites from **100 sites across ten categories** and explain their credibility rankings. **Design look appeared in 46.1% of coded comments**, compared with **28.5%** for information design/structure and **25.1%** for information focus.

These are overlapping mention rates, including positive and negative comments—not percentages of trust caused by each feature. “Design look” was a broad category spanning several visual properties. The paper's example denominator was **2440 comments**, insufficiently reconciled with completion and comment-prompt totals; it should not be equated with 2684 participants.

Category-specific comments also differed: information bias appeared in **30.2%** of news comments versus **11.6%** overall, while sponsor identity appeared in **28.9%** of nonprofit comments versus **8.8%** overall. These comparisons are descriptive, without reported inferential tests.

The study identifies reported reasons in a judgment task. It neither independently manipulates visual design nor estimates the credibility improvement from a redesign. Comments mix noticing with interpretation and cannot reveal the entire judgment process.

## Practical Use

- Make warranted trust assessable: relevant evidence, authorship, affiliations, and accountability must be findable and interpretable.
- Use visual hierarchy to support inspection of substantive information rather than substitute polish for evidence.
- Match credibility cues to the person's task and stakes; not everything can be prominent simultaneously.
- Evaluate what people notice and what they infer separately, then compare perceived trust with justified trust.

Like the [aesthetic–usability effect](/vault/aesthetic-usability-effect.md), this principle concerns appearance-based judgment without certifying underlying quality. Neither finding licenses treating appeal as a substitute for evidence.

## Limitations

The study reflects **2002** websites and a nonrepresentative, largely US recruitment scheme. Assigned comparisons rewarded by charitable donations need not resemble personally consequential decisions. Reduced involvement was proposed as an explanation for reliance on appearance, not experimentally established.

Retrospective comments can omit observations or rationalize judgments. Coding reliability and denominators are insufficiently specified to reconstruct uncertainty. The exact historical rates are not current benchmarks, causal weights, or a universal credibility checklist.
